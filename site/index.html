Case Study

Which pages should an editor review first?

Status: Framing + baseline + model Data: 30K pages · 32 clients Split: Client-holdout Run: Sept 9, 2026

What I was trying to solve

A content team has 30,000 pages and can't review them all. The obvious shortcut: sort by age, review the oldest ones first. I wanted to know — does that actually work?

Short answer: no. On the data I had, it was worse than picking pages at random.

The data

FlyRank gave me 30,000 anonymized pages from 32 clients. 44 columns — search impressions, clicks, CTR, content age, word count, position, engagement. No client names, URLs, or titles. Clean for analysis.

One thing I had to be careful about: the label (is_declining_label) is built from trend_direction. If I let that column leak into the features, the model would cheat. I excluded trend_direction and trend_pct from every feature set.

The baseline that failed

I wrote a simple rule: sort by days_since_last_update (oldest first), then by impressions_90d (most visible first). This is what a team would do without ML.

It got Precision@50 = 0.240. The base rate is 0.542 — meaning if you picked 50 pages blindly, you'd find declining pages in 27 of them. The rule found 12. It was worse than random.

That's the finding I care about. Not the model's score — the baseline's failure. If the easy rule doesn't work, the model has a real job to do.

The model

I trained three models on 52 features (log-transformed activity metrics, content age, CTR, position, engagement rates, plus one-hot encoded categorical tiers). Client-holdout split — no client appears in both train and test.

Random forest won.

What I won't claim

  • The trend window overlaps with the 90-day activity metrics. This is decision-support, not a prospective prediction. I can't tell you a page will decline — only that it currently is.
  • The label measures observed decline, not refresh need. A page might be losing traffic for reasons a content update can't fix (algorithm change, seasonality, competition).
  • I haven't tested this on a separately collected dataset. The client-holdout split is the best generalization check I have, but it's not a live trial.

Method

How I work

Four things I do on every project. They're not fancy — they're just what stops me from reporting a result I can't defend.

I always build a baseline first

Before touching any model, I write the simplest rule I can think of — the kind a non-technical person would build. Then I measure it on the same metric I'll use for the model.

Here, the stale-first rule got Precision@50 = 0.240. Base rate was 0.542. The baseline was worse than random. That's not a failure — it's the whole point. If the easy thing doesn't work, the model has a reason to exist.

I hunt for leakage before training

The label in this dataset is trend_direction == "down". If I let trend_direction or trend_pct into the features, the model would see the answer during training. I checked every column against the label definition and excluded anything label-derived.

I also split by client — 80% of clients for training, 20% for testing. No client in both. This means the model can't memorize "client X always declines" and exploit it at test time.

I'm honest about where AI helped

AI helped me write boilerplate pandas code, draft docstrings, and pressure-test my framing. It did not make the analytical decisions. I chose the baseline, I caught the leakage risk, I rewrote the claim when the original "3× improvement" turned out to be inherited from the starter repo, not measured by me.
