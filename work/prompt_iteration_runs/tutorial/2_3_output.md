# The Language of the Light

Maren had kept the lighthouse at Greystone Point for eleven years, and in all that time, the light had never surprised her.

She knew its rhythm the way she knew her own heartbeat — the slow, mechanical rotation, the four-second interval, the dependable sweep across the black water that said, simply, *here is the danger, here is the shore.* The light was a machine. It did not improvise.

So when she climbed the iron stairs on the fourth night of October and saw that the beam was stuttering, her first thought was mechanical failure. She checked the motor housing. She checked the lens assembly. She checked every bolt and wire she knew by name. Everything was exactly as it should be.

The light continued to stutter.

She pressed her face to the glass and stared out at the water. There — far offshore, almost at the edge of visibility — a dim point of light was blinking. Not the running lights of a vessel. Not the steady glow of another installation. This was deliberate. Rhythmic. A pattern.

And the lighthouse was answering it.

Maren sat down on the cold metal floor of the lantern room and tried to reason through this. She was a practical woman. She had weathered fourteen storms and one appendectomy performed on the mainland by a surgeon who smelled faintly of coffee. She did not believe in things that could not be explained. But she also could not explain this.

She pulled out the small notebook she kept for weather observations and began transcribing the pattern she could see offshore. Long flash, short flash, short flash. Pause. Long, long, short. She watched for twenty minutes until her eyes burned, filling pages with dashes and dots, and then she compared it to the lighthouse's own irregular pulses, which she had also been recording.

They were not identical signals. They were a conversation.

---

She found the code in the archive room the following morning, in a logbook dated 1923. Her predecessor's predecessor, a keeper named Aldric Voss, had written in cramped, meticulous script about a system he had developed with the crew of a research vessel called *The Meridian.* The ship had spent three winters in the waters off Greystone Point, charting the deep-ocean trench that ran several miles offshore. Voss and the ship's navigator, a woman named Petra Halm, had invented their own shorthand — a modified Morse, private and compact — to exchange weather data, observations, small jokes, ordinary human words across the dark water when the radio failed, which it often did.

The logbook ended in February of 1924. There was no explanation for why.

Maren spent two days translating her transcriptions. The message from offshore, assembled slowly from her notebook pages, read: *TRENCH WARMING. CURRENT SHIFTED NORTH. WHEN DID YOU FIRST NOTICE.*

The lighthouse's responses, which she had not programmed, which no living person had programmed, read: *WATER TEMPERATURE ANOMALY LOGGED SIX WEEKS AGO. BIOLUMINESCENCE INCREASED. ARE YOU SEEING THE SAME.*

She sat with this for a long time.

She was a practical woman. She decided to respond.

---

Using the manual override panel — a legacy system she had never touched — she cut into the automatic rotation and sent her own transmission that night. She had studied Voss's code carefully enough to manage simple sentences. *WHO IS TRANSMITTING,* she sent. *THIS IS THE GREYSTONE KEEPER. IDENTIFY YOURSELF.*

The answer came within minutes. *AUTONOMOUS BUOY STATION MERIDIAN-7. INSTALLED 2019. SOLAR POWERED. LINKED TO DEEP OCEAN SENSOR ARRAY. YOUR LIGHT ESTABLISHED CONTACT FORTY-THREE DAYS AGO. WE ASSUMED AUTOMATED RESPONSE SYSTEM.*

Maren laughed out loud in the empty lantern room. The sound bounced off the glass and came back to her, and she laughed again.

The lighthouse had not been haunted. It had not been enchanted. It had been doing what it was always built to do — sending information across dark water to anything that was listening. Somewhere in its old electrical bones, in circuits laid down by Aldric Voss himself, the lighthouse had recognized a familiar frequency and begun speaking in the only private language it knew.

She sent back: *NO AUTOMATED SYSTEM. JUST ME. TELL ME ABOUT THE TRENCH.*

What came back over the following hour was a detailed account of rising water temperatures along the ocean floor, a northward shift in a deep current that had been stable for decades, and unprecedented thermal venting in three locations. The buoy's sensors were precise and alarming. This was exactly the kind of data that researchers needed and often struggled to collect consistently. The kind of data that rarely made it from the middle of the ocean to anyone who could act on it.

Maren spent the rest of the night writing everything down.

---

In the morning, she filed a formal report with the coastal research authority, attaching her transcriptions, the translated coordinates from the buoy, and a careful explanation of the communication method. She also contacted the historical society about Aldric Voss and Petra Halm. She felt, without being able to justify it entirely, that someone should know their code was still in use.

The researchers arrived by boat ten days later, excited and disbelieving and then just excited. They set up proper receiving equipment in the watch room. They began a formal data-sharing protocol with Meridian-7.

Before they overwrote the lighthouse's manual settings with newer software, Maren asked for one hour alone in the lantern room.

She used the override panel to send a final message in the old code. Not to the buoy. Just outward, generally, into the dark.

*GREYSTONE LIGHT,* she sent. *STILL HERE. STILL WATCHING.*

She stood at the glass and looked at the black water for a long time.

No answer came. But then, sometimes the most important thing a light can do is simply keep shining, and trust that someone out there is paying attention.
